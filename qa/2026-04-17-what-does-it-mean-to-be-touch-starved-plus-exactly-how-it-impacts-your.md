What does it mean to be touch-starved?

You won’t see ‘touch-starved’ or ‘touch-hungry’ in your biology textbook. “They aren’t scientific terms, so there aren’t formal, established definitions for them,” says Justin Lehmiller, PhD, a social psychologist, research fellow at The Kinsey Institute, and author of Tell Me What You Want: The Science of Sexual Desire and How It Can Help You Improve Your Sex Life.

But colloquially, touch starvation refers to a lack of physical contact in an individual's life. Sometimes known as touch deprivation, when an individual is touch starved, they are yearning for human-to-human contact, whether it’s a hand on your shoulder, or a cuddle on the couch, says Spann.

By contrast, someone who is touch-hungry wants affection, but hasn’t yet gotten to the place of feeling deprived of it, says Spann. “Someone who is touch or skin hungry might have had touch recently, for example, but is seeking it again to meet that need.”

Indeed, these two terms are closely related and often used interchangeably, notes relationship expert and therapist Jor-El Caraballo, LMHC, author of Self-Care of Black Men. But more important than their precise definition is the awareness they create; the terms give language for the importance of touch in an individual’s emotional, psychological, and physical well-being.

The Health Effects of Being Touch-Starved

Feeling touch-starved doesn’t just leave you craving connection—it can cascade through every system in your body causing psychological, emotional, and physical consequences, according to experts.

One reason touch is so powerful is its effect on your brain chemistry. Intentional touch triggers the release of a whoosh of feel-good neurochemicals into our system, including oxytocin, the bonding hormone, and serotonin, the happiness hormone, says Caraballo. Together, this cocktail of chemicals fosters feelings of connection, safety, and belonging that can feel missing in the absence of affectionate touch.

Touch also plays a critical role in regulating the nervous system. Skin-to-skin contact activates the parasympathetic nervous system—the part of your body responsible for “rest and digest”—helping counterbalance the stress response. “Without touch, it’s more difficult to manage stress,” says Caraballo. Over time, this imbalance can lead to increased blood pressure, a higher resting heart rate, and contribute to muscle tension, chronic aches, and headaches, he says. Prolonged stress can also manifest physically as digestive issues, hair loss, and even hormonal disruptions such as lower testosterone levels and erectile dysfunction, per the American Psychological Association.

Touch deprivation may even interfere with immune response. A 2024 review in Nature Human Behavior of more than 13,000 participants found that touch interventions (like massage, gentle stroking, and hugging) have significant physical health benefits, including regulating cortisol levels. Since chronically high cortisol levels can suppress immune response, the researchers determined that touch can support immune resilience.

Signs You Might Be Touch-Starved

Unlike gonorrhea or low testosterone, touch starvation can’t be diagnosed with a blood draw, urine test, or brain scan. The easiest way to tell if you’re experiencing skin hunger is to check in with yourself, taking inventory of your relationship happiness, mental and emotional well-being, and your overall sense of connection.

“When people aren’t getting their need for intimate touch met, their relationship quality and mental health and well-being can suffer,” says Lehmiller. So, if you’re feeling persistently lonely, emotionally disconnected, or craving closeness, a lack of intentional, caring touch may be playing a role, he says.

In Western societies, men are taught that all touch is sexual touch, explains Caraballo. “This norm leads all boys and men to avoid touching and hugging their friends for fear of it communicating some sexual desire or hidden interest,” he says. That fear can be especially intense for boys and men who aren’t straight and want to conceal that part of their identity, Caraballo says. And for those living in more conservative or homophobic environments, the pressure to limit affectionate touch—even among close male friends—can be even stronger.

Single men may be especially at risk for a lack of touch. “Given that touch is largely seen as a way to build connection in romantic and intimate relationships, men who are in relationships more routinely have an outlet for their desire for physical connection,” says Caraballo. Single men—especially those who do not live close to their family of origin or chosen family—may not have outlets for meeting their physical affection needs, he says.

Even those having one-night stands may still feel touch-deprived. “The challenging part of random hookups and one-night stands is that they produce a very short-lived positive response, which will lead to the demand for more touch soon thereafter,” says Caraballo. “At times, it can also leave men feeling worse because it doesn’t quite produce the desired intimacy they are seeking psychologically, or the non-sexual physical touch actually necessary to keep us feeling connected, healthy, and less stressed,” he says.

How to Cope If You’re Touch-Starved

The good news? There are ways to incorporate touch into your life in a way that feels safe, affirming, and welcome for all involved.

If you’re partnered, Lehmiller recommends starting with non-sexual touch. “Some men only touch their partners when they want sex, which can lead their partners to shy away from touch because, rather than touch promoting relaxation and connection, it starts to feel like pressure to have sex,” he says. Instead of going straight for sucking and shagging, try inviting your partner to cuddle on the couch, hold hands, or share a bath.

Even better is to talk openly with your partner about what you need. “After thinking about the type of touch you’re craving, try to be yourself, use terminology and words that feel authentic to you, and invite your partner to join you in the touch that feels best,” says Spann. Vulnerability and a low-pressure invitation can go a long way in creating a deeper connection, he says.

If you’re single—or want to expand your sources of touch—consider safe, non-romantic outlets.

For some men, getting a professional massage or paying for other care services like a haircut, manicure, or sports massage can scratch the itch, says Spann. Depending on where you live, there might be a community of folks who are also actively seeking touch through cuddle parties, sex parties, and even dancing, he says.
