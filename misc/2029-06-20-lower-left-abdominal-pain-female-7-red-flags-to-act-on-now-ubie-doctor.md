What causes lower left abdominal pain in a female?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Lower left abdominal pain in women has many possible causes, ranging from digestive issues to gynecologic, urinary, and musculoskeletal conditions.

Common causes of lower left abdominal pain in women include:

Gastrointestinal: diverticulitis, IBS, IBD, constipation, colitis, or bowel obstruction

Gynecologic: ovarian cysts, ovarian torsion, ectopic pregnancy, endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), or ovulation pain (mittelschmerz)

Urinary: urinary tract infection (UTI), kidney stones, or pyelonephritis

Other: hernias, muscle strain, or vascular problems

Seek emergency care if you experience: sudden severe pain, high fever, persistent vomiting, abnormal vaginal bleeding, fainting, or any abdominal pain during early pregnancy—these may signal a life-threatening condition.

Because the causes vary so widely—and treatment depends entirely on the underlying issue—guessing can delay care or cause unnecessary worry. Explanation

What Causes Lower Left Abdominal Pain in a Female?

Lower left abdominal pain in women can arise from a variety of systems: gastrointestinal, gynecological, urinary, vascular, and musculoskeletal. While many causes are minor and resolve on their own, some require prompt medical attention. This guide reviews common and serious causes, drawing on the latest research and clinical expertise.

Gastrointestinal Causes

Diverticulitis

Inflammation or infection of small pouches (diverticula) in the colon wall.

Typically presents with steady pain in the left lower quadrant, fever, constipation or diarrhea.

Strate & Morris (2019) report an increasing incidence of diverticulitis, especially in adults over 50.

Treatment ranges from antibiotics and diet modification to, rarely, surgery.

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

Functional disorder causing cramping, bloating, gas, and changes in bowel habits.

[Act On](https://pepvoice.com/)

Pain often improves after a bowel movement.

No structural or lab abnormalities on testing.

Constipation

Hard, infrequent stools stretch the colon, causing discomfort low on the left side.

Diet low in fiber, dehydration, or medications can contribute.

Increasing fluids, fiber, and gentle laxatives usually help.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

Ulcerative colitis often involves the left colon and rectum, causing pain, urgent diarrhea, sometimes bloody.

Crohn's disease can affect any part of the GI tract but may localize to the sigmoid colon.

Colonic Obstruction or Volvulus

Partial blockage or twisting of the sigmoid colon leads to severe pain, distension, nausea, and vomiting.

Requires urgent imaging and often surgery.

Colitis (Infectious or Ischemic)

Inflammation from infection (e.g., C. difficile) or reduced blood flow to the colon.

[Doctor's Note](https://metabolic-horizon.bbsxyxy.com/)

Presents with crampy pain, urgent diarrhea, sometimes bloody stools.

Needs prompt evaluation and treatment.

Gynecological Causes

Ovarian Cysts

Fluid-filled sacs on the ovary; common and often asymptomatic.

Larger cysts can cause dull or sharp pain if they rupture or bleed.

Ovarian Torsion

Twisting of the ovary cuts off blood supply, causing sudden, severe unilateral pain, nausea, vomiting.

[Female Red](https://ageless-peptides.china-akan.com/)

Surgical emergency to save the ovary.

Ectopic Pregnancy

Fertilized egg implants outside the uterus, often in a fallopian tube.

Sharp, stabbing pain, often with vaginal bleeding and dizziness.

Life-threatening; requires urgent evaluation.

Endometriosis

Uterine lining tissue grows outside the uterus, causing cyclical pain, especially around menstruation.

Pain may radiate to the lower back or thighs.

Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

Infection of the reproductive organs, often from sexually transmitted bacteria.

Pain is usually bilateral but can start on one side; fever, vaginal discharge, pain with intercourse.

Mittelschmerz ("Middle Pain")

Ovulation pain occurring mid-cycle, typically mild and self-limited.

Usually resolves within a day or two.

Urinary Causes

Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)

Infection of the bladder (cystitis) can cause suprapubic or lower abdominal discomfort, burning with urination, frequent urge to urinate.

Simple UTIs are treated with antibiotics.

Kidney Stones

Stones in the left ureter can cause intense, colicky flank pain that radiates to the groin or lower abdomen.

Associated with nausea, vomiting, blood in the urine.

Pyelonephritis

Kidney infection causing flank pain, fever, chills, nausea, sometimes lower quadrant pain if the lower pole is involved.

Requires prompt antibiotic treatment.

Musculoskeletal & Other Causes

Muscle Strain: Overuse or sudden twisting can injure abdominal wall muscles, causing localized pain.

Hernias: Left-sided inguinal or femoral hernias may manifest as bulge and discomfort, especially with lifting or straining.

[Female Red](https://reboundworks.escortskhi.com/)

Nerve Entrapment: Intercostal or ilioinguinal nerves can irritate the abdominal wall, mimicking internal pain.

Vascular: Rarely, conditions like segmental colitis or mesenteric ischemia present with localized pain; usually in older adults with vascular disease.

When to Seek Medical Attention

Most mild causes improve with rest, hydration, dietary changes, or over-the-counter pain relief. However, you should call your doctor or seek emergency care if you experience any of the following:

Sudden, severe pain that prevents you from sitting still or finding a comfortable position

Fever above 38°C (100.4°F)

Persistent vomiting or inability to keep liquids down

Signs of dehydration (dizziness, dry mouth, low urine output)

Blood in the stool or urine

Vaginal bleeding not related to your normal menstrual cycle

Swelling or a noticeable lump in the groin or lower abdomen

Chest pain, shortness of breath, or fainting

Summary of Key Points

Gastrointestinal issues (diverticulitis, IBS, IBD, obstruction) and gynecologic conditions (ovarian cysts, ectopic pregnancy, endometriosis) are common culprits.

Urinary tract problems (UTIs, kidney stones) and musculoskeletal injuries also cause left-side pain.

Mild cases often resolve with conservative measures.

Seek prompt care for severe, persistent, or worsening symptoms.
