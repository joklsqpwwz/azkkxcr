It even got corporations thinking, which is part of the reason Chief Purpose Officer is now an actual job title. And top meditation and sleep app Calm just hired coach Jay Shetty to fill that role for its company.

Shetty, if you haven’t already encountered him through the Daily Jay, a 7-minute meditation series, his bestselling Think Like a Monk book, his On Purpose podcast, or the other mental wellness initiatives he’s involved in, graduated from Cass Business School in the UK…and then trained as a monk for three years. He's known for his down-to-earth, actionable advice around topics like purpose—that thing that experts insist is so important for our lifespans, health, and well-being but can seem so elusive. We caught up with him to find out more about how purpose dovetails with success and how we can break down “finding your purpose” into something doable. His insights on purpose, meaning, success, and even money now:

The idea of a Chief Purpose Officer is pretty intriguing. Should we all aim to be Chief Purpose Officers in our lives?

I find that many people come to a place where they find that what they're doing every day is not meaningful or purposeful. It may be what their parents wanted them to do. It may be something they thought they dreamed of doing. It may have just been the smartest, safest, most stable option at school or at university or college. And then after a while they start thinking, I want to do something different.

I think that's the point that we choose to become Chief Purpose Officers of our own lives. Because up until then, we've very much been in the passenger seat of our lives. We've often been driven by other people's decisions, other people's opinions, expectations, or obligations or pressures. And I think the day you accept that and say, I'm no longer going to live the life that I think other people want me to live, but I want to live the life that I want to live, that is the day we become Chief Purpose Officers of our life.

That’s scary as heck, though. If you’re not used to driving, and realize the car is going in the wrong direction, how do you take the wheel?

There are a few steps here. The first step is to learn to like where you are, even if you don't love it. The way we do that is by recognizing that even if what we're doing right now doesn't feel like our purpose, there are skills to be gained.

Learn anything and everything that is interesting to you and be curious about in the place you’re in. That may require you to go a bit outside of your day-to-day job in your workplace and have conversations with people in different departments. But that mindset of continuous learning is going to benefit you no matter what you end up doing. Learning that even though what you’re doing isn’t what you want to do long term, you can see the value of what you’re getting there now creates a stable foundation on which to build your purpose. If you try to build your purpose on instability—quitting your job because you hate it or because you’re feeling regret and pain going into work every day—that is a hard place to be the launchpad of purpose.

So what are we really defining as purpose?

[Other People's](https://skinsequence.escortskhi.com/)

If you had an equation for purpose, it would be passion plus strengths plus contribution. The way I often describe the distinction between passion and purpose is that your passion makes you happy, but your purpose makes other people happy.

So now when you have that equation, just as you would in a math class, you start trying to solve different parts of the equation. You get three very simple questions: What is my passion? What are my strengths? And then how do I want to contribute? You can do them in any order.

You might approach finding your purpose by starting with your passion. Then the next step is asking yourself, “do I want to get good at this?” And I think this is a step that has often been missed. We often say if you have a passion for what you’re doing, then you’re going to be so happy and life's going to be great and everything's going to work out. And I would disagree with that. I would say that, if you have a great passion, that's good. The question is whether that is something you’re willing to get good at, get training in, and put the hours in for? If not, it can be a wonderful hobby.

And then the third and final aspect of the equation is how do I use that to serve others? So let's say I love travel. I love photography. I take these incredible pictures of some of the most phenomenal landscapes in the world. I may sell them to blogs and websites and magazines. How does that serve people? Because you share them with people who may not be able to visit them. You share them with people to give them advice on how to get the most out of them. So now that you're using that skill and that passion of yours to improve the lives of other people, that's when it truly becomes a purpose.

How do you know if your purpose is “good enough,” especially if it’s not something like saving the world from hunger?

A purpose is anything that positively improves the lives of others. For example, I used to have this bus driver that picked us up for school every day, almost as if he were our dad. He was so loving and kind, and very much made the bus, a safe environment and was always asking people about how school was and how they were doing. One could say that that didn't have a big impact because not that many people got on the bus every day. But he had a positive impact on my life, and I still remember him. He’s improving everyone’s life by using the tools, the skills, and the capacity he had, and that, to me, is a purpose.

You can extend and scale purpose as much as you want or keep it where it is today. Our own capacity is something only we can decide, and no one really has the right to judge someone’s purpose as big or small. I don’t think we should be deterred from a purpose because someone else values it or not. If you are improving one person's life, it's amazing.

But what if you find your passion and purpose and pursue it in your career and then find out that the path you chose isn’t that great?

There is no ideal situation, and accepting that in the beginning is probably the healthiest thing. There's always going to be something that you have to do, or need to do, even when you're doing mostly what you want to do. And I think that the journey we're on is not binary, it's percentages.

You have things you have to do, and you have things you want to do. We should always be moving in a direction closer to doing more of what we want to do, but we should not have the delusion to think that that means we'll never have to do what we have to do.

If right now your life is 10 percent what you want to do, 50 percent what you need to do, and 40 percent what you have to do, then how can you move up that 10 percent to 25 percent this year, or 35 percent? That’s the journey I’d like people to take rather than this leap of needing it to be 100 percent, which won’t happen. You’re going to find discrepancies, even when you try to do the greatest work in the world. I think expecting that perfection holds us back from starting the process and making progress. That imperfection forces us to grow more—if everything were in a perfect state, you’d never have to grow.
