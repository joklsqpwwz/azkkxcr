Joel Relampagos, now 41, was at the height of his career. He was executive producing hit television shows—including The Biggest Loser—and living the glamorous Hollywood life. With that lifestyle came a price: he was drinking—a lot. He would often wake up surrounded by bottles, unaware of his actions. After 17 years of drinking, Relampagos realized alcohol was going to kill him if he continued to let it infiltrate his life. In 2019, he took the first step to getting sober.

Had Relampagos not mustered up the courage to ask for help so many years ago, he may not be alive today. He's been sober for over 4 years now, and still utilizes the tips and tricks he wrote down during his time in rehab to process his emotions, rather than reaching for the bottle. He has since won an Emmy for his most recent project, Recipe for Change, and has started a nonprofit called Change Your Algorithm, which is focused on providing the community with free mental health guidance. You can follow along with more of Relampagos's journey, here.

Here's how Relampagos turned his life around, in his own words.

I WAS BORN in the Philippines. I moved out here to the U.S. when I was six. Being an immigrant, there was a lot of shame that I grew up with. Not just because I was a person of color living in a community where I felt very different, but also because I was in the closet.

I felt like I had to live up to a lot of expectations in my household as the only son—to eventually have wife and kids, and become a doctor. I did not like the sight of blood at all. So I [thought], I'm totally screwed here. Luckily, in college, I decided to just live my truth. And I saw that MTV was casting for a show called True Life: I'm Coming Out.

[Finally Asked for Help](https://benpepsi.com/)

For that show, I came out to my family on camera. It was the craziest, but also the best thing I'd ever done, because it just showed me the importance of telling my story and being authentic to who I am. It also got me my first job in entertainment as a production assistant for the Grammy Awards.

Being in entertainment was so perfect for me, because I grew up with that of shame and need for validation. I ended up in this industry that was about ratings and getting people to validate your work, and thus, validating you. But, I never really received self-validation. So I turned to alcohol, which was really encouraged in Hollywood. While I had a very successful career in entertainment in the daytime, I was very heavy drinker at night.

At the time, it felt like it was filling the void of not dealing with my emotions, past traumas, and the [lack of] validation I got growing up. I just kept drinking more and more, to the point where I thought, "I'm gonna die." I was ruining relationships. My depression got worse, my anxiety got worse. I didn't feel like I had a purpose in life. Despite having all this money, despite having this amazing career, I still felt really empty.

The moment where I felt like I needed to ask for help was a little over four years ago. I woke up at two in the afternoon, in the middle of empty bottles of wine everywhere. All bottles that I had drank. I didn't throw a party. It was literally just a party for one: me.

[Was Killing Him](https://incretin-path.escortskhi.com/)

Step one was asking for help. I called my dad. In a Filipino household, you don't talk about your emotions. You don't ask for help. So, I was terrified. But, I really felt like I had nothing to lose by asking for help. I had to set aside my ego and my pride. That false narrative of "boys don't cry" had to go out the door. I had to redefine what vulnerability meant to me. That was really the moment when I went, "I get it. Asking for help is actually a form of strength, not weakness." And luckily, he was very compassionate, and helped me find a rehab facility.

It really brought me back to when I came out on national TV. The younger Joel was so wanting to be his authentic self. I thought that was so cool. I chose to be me back then, and find courage, even though it was really uncomfortable. Then, I started to lose who I was, [and] I gave booze like the steering wheel. And so I thought about how my authenticity had kind of taken the passenger seat, and that gave I needed to gain my power back.

"I started to LOSE who I was, [and] I gave booze the STEERING WHEEL. And so I thought about how my AUTHENTICITY had kind of taken the PASSENGER SEAT, and that gave me what I needed to gain MY POWER back."

Rehab was the best, except for the first week. I had to let go of most of my control—and I am a control freak. The first thought in my head was I need a drink. I remember looking at the fence wondering if I could hop them. But, I resisted it. What I loved the most was the back to back group therapy. Being a Filipino immigrant, I knew nothing about mental wellness or talking about my emotions. It felt like I was in a university about myself, where I was learning about my mind and how it works. I journaled every day to help absorb everything I was taught, culminating in hundreds of pages of notes. What really healed me was understanding my traumas and how to process them.

The number one thing I learned is that I am the observer of my thoughts—I'm not actually my thoughts. Just because I wanted a drink, didn't mean that I have to go get a drink. I could instead sit back and observe why I was feeling that way. Feeling is healing. Pain is inevitable, but suffering is optional. I realized I was causing myself more suffering by drinking through the pain.

[Finally Asked for Help](https://cortexcompass.jcesqc8.com/)

Now, when I go out and everyone around me is drinking, my pattern will go, 'oh, I should have a drink, too'. And then I observed my thoughts. And then I think, I don't need a drink. Because I've seen that movie—and it's the worst movie I've ever seen. I started to do mental reversals like these for trigger moments in my life, and it's helped how I react in the moment.

I've started to workout everyday, too. It has just been my therapy and that's how I get my dopamine, endorphins, oxytocin, serotonin—all the chemicals that I would get from alcohol. Working out has kind of been my best therapist.

Overall, I've learned that faith and fear are two things we can't see, so I decided I'm going to start putting my energy towards the one that can evolve me. I started to tell myself that change is possible and that I'm capable of becoming the person I deserve to be.
